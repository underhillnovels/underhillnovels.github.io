Chapter 1

Proof of Service

Jessica Heart had always wanted to be a criminal lawyer.

She had said so at sixteen, in a demountable that smelled of warm photocopier, and the careers teacher had written it on a card and underlined it twice. Nobody had asked her what she thought the job was.

At twenty past nine she was in the robing room at the Magistrates Court with the blouse out of the bag, because she had stopped trusting a blouse to a hanger in a car. The good black suit. The shoes she could not walk to the car park in, which lived in her bag beside the shoes she could. Hair up, hard, with the pins in the same four places they had sat since she was twenty-three. Lipstick, because without it men asked her twice whether she was feeling all right.

It took forty minutes every morning. It had cost her about nine days a year for fourteen years and she had worked that out once, at a conference, during a session on wellbeing.

Court 3 was cold at the front and airless at the back. Sergeant Kilby had the prosecution table and two cardboard boxes and the look of a man who had been given the list that morning. Her client sat down beside her and put his phone face down on the bench without being asked, which she had told him to do in the corridor.

Dale Prewer was thirty-one and had come in a shirt with a collar. He had done that himself; she had not had to ask. It would help.

One count. Contravening a family violence order. Fifty metres, and he had been forty, in a car park at Glenorchy, on a Tuesday afternoon in March.

The complainant was in the gallery on the left with another woman beside her. Jess looked at her once, at the start.

Kelly Vautin was twenty-six. Flat shoes, a good coat, both hands on the strap of her bag. She had been crying in the corridor and had stopped, and had done something about her face before she came in. She would have been all right in the box. She would have been clear, and she would have stayed clear under cross, and the magistrate would have believed every word of it.

Jess turned back to her own table and opened the file.

* * *

The order had been made on the eleventh of February, in his absence, in a list of nineteen.

She had found it on the Sunday night, with the file on the kitchen bench and a glass of wine going warm beside it. It had taken four minutes.

Sergeant Kilby took it the way he was always going to take it. The order existed. The order had been served. There was a record of service. There was a note from Constable Whelan of a telephone call on the thirteenth, in which Mr Prewer was told that an order had been made.

"Sergeant," Jess said. "Constable Whelan's note. Does it record what he told Mr Prewer the order required him to do?"

Kilby read it again. He took his time about it, which she respected.

"It records that he was advised an order was in place."

"That's not what I asked. Does it record any condition? Any distance? Any address?"

"No."

"Is Constable Whelan here?"

"Constable Whelan left the service in August."

"And the record of service — that's the document at tab four. Whose signature is on it?"

A pause while he found it.

"It's not signed."

"It's not signed," Jess said, and sat down.

She made the submission standing, with one hand flat on the bar table, and it took her under three minutes. Knowledge of the order was not knowledge of its terms. The Crown could prove that Mr Prewer knew a piece of paper existed. It could not prove that he knew what was written on it, and a man cannot knowingly contravene a condition that nobody can establish was ever communicated to him.

She did not say one thing in that courtroom that was untrue.

The magistrate took eleven minutes. He was not happy about it and he said so, in the careful way they say it, and then he dismissed the complaint.

* * *

Prewer put his hand on her forearm in the corridor and left it there a second longer than she wanted.

"You beauty," he said. "You absolute beauty."

"Mr Prewer. The order is still in force. It doesn't go away because this charge did. If you go near her again you'll be back here and the next one won't have a hole in it."

"Yeah, no. Understood." He was already looking past her at the lift. "Hundred per cent."

Behind him the doors of Court 3 opened and the two women came out and went the other way, towards the stairs, and the one who was not Kelly Vautin had her arm round her.

Jess stood in the corridor with her bag in her hand. The stairwell door swung to and clicked.

* * *

At the office they knew before she got there, because Kilby had told somebody and somebody had told somebody.

Hugh Tyne came out of his room with his glasses up on his head.

"On service?"

"On the note. Whelan never put the conditions in it."

"And the record's unsigned." He laughed, once, through his nose. "God help us. Good girl. That's the third one off them this year."

"Fourth."

"Fourth." He was delighted. "There's a bottle of something in the kitchen from the Ellis matter. Have a glass at five, we'll make a thing of it."

"I might."

"You should. You don't make enough of yourself, Jess, that's your whole trouble."

He went back in. Through the glass she could see him picking up the phone, still pleased. He would tell it twice more before lunch and get it slightly wrong each time.

In her own room she took the shoes off under the desk and put her feet flat on the carpet. There was a sandwich she had bought at eight o'clock and a new brief with a blue band round it that had not been there at half past seven.

She ate the sandwich.

At some point in the afternoon she went to put the Prewer file in the cabinet and found she could not bring up the woman's face from the gallery. Not the coat, not the hands, not the face.

She had been watching the sergeant.

Chapter 2

The Blue Band

The blue band meant legal aid.

Legal aid meant a fixed fee that would cover about a third of the hours, and the firm took its share of them the way every firm did, so that the Commission would keep sending through the work that paid properly. Nobody said no to a grant. What happened instead was that the file went round the building, and people were busy, and it came to rest.

By the time one landed on a desk with the band still on it, two or three people had picked it up and put it down again.

"Wendy's got the Bridgewater thing running till October," Hugh said, in the doorway, not coming in. "And Tom won't touch a persistent. Says he hasn't got the stomach for them any more, which at sixty-one is a thing a man is allowed to say."

"Right."

"He's at Risdon. Remand. You'll want to get out there this week, they're booking a fortnight ahead." He put his hand flat on the doorframe. "It's a dog of a brief, Jess. I'm not pretending otherwise."

"I didn't think you were."

"Good girl."

She did not take it home that night, or the night after. On the Thursday she put it in the boot so that it would not sit on the passenger seat, and drove up Davey Street in the rain.

* * *

The flat was in Battery Point, two rooms and a half, and it had been hers for nine years.

She came in at twenty to seven and did it in the order she always did it. Keys in the bowl. Heating on. Shoes off at the mat, both pairs. Then the bathroom, and the pins out one at a time into the saucer on the shelf, and her hair down, and both hands up into it and the whole weight of the day coming off the back of her head in about four seconds. Then the bra, through the sleeve, which was the best part. Then the good suit on its hanger with the shoulders straightened, because it was eleven hundred dollars and she had three of them.

She ate standing up: rice from Tuesday, an egg on it, pepper. Her sister had sent a photograph of a dog in a cone.

Barney post-op. Twelve days of this. Tell me again why we do it. xx

There was a missed call from Prue at half past five and a voicemail she would listen to tomorrow.

At nine o'clock she went down to the car for the brief.

* * *

Braden Curry was forty-four and had been in Risdon since the fourteenth of June.

She laid it out on the kitchen table the way she always did, which was counter-clockwise from her left hand: charge sheet, chronology, antecedents, the orders, the statements, the medical, the photographs face down at the end. She poured a glass of wine and sat down in front of it.

One count of persistent family violence. Two of assault. Nine contraventions of a family violence order.

The chronology ran to four pages. An interim order in September. A final order in October, this one served on him in open court while he stood there, so there would be no argument about what he knew. A phone call in November. Two hundred and forty calls in December, which the summary gave as a figure because listing them would have taken a page. A car parked across the road from the house in Lindisfarne on the twenty-second of January, for five and a half hours, photographed by a neighbour from an upstairs window with the number plate legible.

He was charged at the end of January and bailed on the ninth of February, on conditions.

Six of the nine contraventions came after that. The last was on the eleventh of June. On the fourteenth he was remanded in custody, and he had been there ever since.

Naomi London was thirty-three. Her statement ran to sixty-one pages and had been taken over three days in March.

It started in 2015. She had been twenty-two and working on the front desk of a dental practice in Rosny, booking appointments and taking payments. He had come in as a patient — three visits over five weeks, for a crown — and on the third visit he had leaned on the counter and asked her out in front of the two other girls on the desk. She had found that flattering at the time. She had put it in the statement without comment.

Jess read the sixty-one pages.

It was not the hitting that did it. The hitting was in there, in paragraphs 140 to 167, dated where she could date them and described in the flat way police take it down, and it was bad. But Jess had read a great many paragraphs like that.

It was paragraph 38. He had not liked her friend Shanae, and so there had been a conversation about Shanae, and then another one, and then over about fourteen months there had stopped being a Shanae. He had never told her not to see her. The statement said that twice.

And paragraph 71, which was about money, and which was four lines long and said that she had not known the password to her own account since 2019 and had not thought about it in years.

Jess put her hand flat on the page and kept it there for a moment.

* * *

The photographs were last because she always put them last.

There were forty-one, in a plastic sleeve, taken by a forensic officer at the Royal on the second of June and again at the station on the ninth. Each one had a scale rule laid beside it, a small white strip marked off in centimetres, so that a jury could be told how big a thing was.

She went through them in order, with the list beside her.

Photographs 1 to 6, face and neck, four angles. Photographs 7 to 11, right upper arm, the grip marks, which the officer had numbered individually. Photograph 12, the same arm at seven days. Photograph 13, left forearm, defensive.

Photograph 14: left hip and lower abdomen. Two older bruises, yellow at the edges, and a small bird on the hipbone, drawn in one unbroken line.

The list had nothing to say about the bird. She looked at it anyway.

It was about the size of a thumbnail and somebody who could draw had done it. It sat in the soft place below the point of the hip, where a waistband would cover it. Nobody would ever see it who had not been invited to.

Jess had never had one. There had been a conversation about it at twenty-two, with three other girls and a bottle of something, and nothing had come of it. She sat at her kitchen table at a quarter to eleven and thought about where she would put one, if she ever did. Not the ankle. Not the shoulder. Somewhere she would have to decide to show.

Then she turned the page.

Photographs 15 to 19, the back of the left thigh. Photograph 20, the sole of the right foot.

She looked at the sole of the right foot for some time.

Then she squared the whole sleeve up on the table and put it at the bottom of the pile, and sat back, and found that the wine was still where she had poured it and had gone the temperature of the room.

She took it to the sink and tipped it out and washed the glass and stood it on the rack.

* * *

At twenty past eleven she opened a new attendance note and dated it.

She typed: Reviewed brief. Client in custody since 14 June. He did it. He did all of it, and the only question on this file is how long he gets.

She sat and looked at that for a while.

Then she took it out, word by word, back to the full stop after 14 June, and wrote: Client to be advised as to the strength of the Crown case and the sentencing range available on a plea at this stage.

She read it again. It was correct. It was the sentence any solicitor in the state would have written and there was nothing in it that anybody could object to.

She saved it, and shut the laptop, and went back to the table, and read the sixty-one pages a second time.

Chapter 3

Professional Visit

She had a bra she only wore to prisons.

It was grey and had no wire in it and it was eight years old, and it lived folded at the back of the drawer with the thermals, and twice a year she put it on at half past seven in the morning and remembered why she had bought it. Flat shoes. Trousers or a skirt below the knee, nothing that needed adjusting. A cardigan, because the rooms were cold and because sleeves were simpler. No necklace. Nothing in her pockets.

It had taken her about two years, when she was young, to notice that none of it applied to the men she went out there with. They put on whatever they had on.

The drive took twenty-five minutes. Over the bridge and north along the eastern shore with the river flat and grey on her left, then up, and the country opened out brown with the fence running over the top of the hill.

In the car park she put her phone and her bag in the boot and locked the car and walked across with the key and the clear folder in her hand.

* * *

Getting in took thirty-five minutes.

Practising certificate. The form, which asked her to declare things she had not brought. The locker, for the car key. The detector, and then the wand, held by an officer about her own age who said sorry twice and did not need to.

Then the first door, and the wait between the two doors, which was the part she had never got used to — six or eight seconds in a space the size of a lift with nothing happening and nothing to look at.

The interview room had a table fixed to the floor, two chairs, a window in the door and a smell of floor polish over something else. She sat with her back to the window and set the folder square in front of her.

They brought him at twenty past ten.

* * *

Braden Curry was a big man gone soft through the shoulders, with grey coming in at the sides and a forearm tattoo from a long time ago. He had shaved. He came in and waited by the chair until she said sit down, and then he sat.

"Mrs Heart."

"Ms. Jessica Heart. Legal Aid have given your file to our firm, so I'm your solicitor from here on."

"Right. Right." He put both hands on the table and then took them off again. "I'd offer you a cup of tea."

"That's all right."

"No, I know. I just — you say it, don't you. Someone comes in." He looked at the table. "Sorry."

She opened the folder.

"Mr Curry, before we start. Has anybody been out to see you about this?"

"There was a bloke in July. Young fella. Half an hour." He thought about it. "I've been here four months and nobody's sat down and told me what I'm actually charged with. I know what it's called. I don't know what it means."

"All right. We'll do that today."

"Thank you," he said, and he meant it, and she wrote the date at the top of the page.

* * *

She took him through it properly. The count of persistent family violence and what the Crown had to prove to make it out. The two assaults. The nine contraventions, and the difference between the three before bail and the six after.

He followed all of it.

"Say I put my hand up to the contraventions," he said. "Just them. Can that be done on its own, or is it the whole lot or nothing?"

Nobody had told him to ask that.

"It can be done. Whether it helps you is another question."

"Because of the six."

"Because of the six."

"Yeah." He rubbed his jaw. "Yeah. That's what does me, isn't it. Not the rest. The rest they'd have given me a bond for, first time up. It's that I kept going."

"That's broadly right."

He had a refrigeration business at Cambridge. Nine on the books, four trucks, twenty-two years. He wanted to know who was paying the men. He wanted to know whether his brother-in-law could sign on the account, and whether that would look bad, and he was relieved when she said it would not.

"They're good blokes," he said. "Dean's got three kids. I'm not having Dean go without because of me."

"Nobody's suggesting he will."

"No. No, I know."

When she came to character references he said: "You could talk to my first wife if you wanted. Kerry. We're still on good terms, she'd say I was all right." Jess wrote K. Curry — refs? in the margin and turned the page.

* * *

An hour in, he asked about Naomi.

"Can I ask you something. Is she all right?"

"Mr Curry—"

"I know I'm not meant to ask. I'm not asking where she is or anything like that, I'm not stupid." He opened both hands on the table. "I just want to know if someone's looking after her."

"I can't discuss the complainant with you."

"Fair enough."

He sat back. For a while he looked at the window in the door, where an officer went past and did not look in.

"She's not well," he said. "That's the thing nobody's put in that file. She hasn't been well for years and I've been the one holding it together, and I've said it to the doctor and I've said it to her mother and I've said it to the police, and not one of them has ever listened to me about it."

"Mr Curry."

"I'm not saying it to get out of anything."

"I understand. But I need you to hear this." She put the pen down. "If that becomes your defence, it will go very badly. Juries have heard it. It is the thing they expect to hear, and the moment you say it they stop listening to everything else."

He took that in. He nodded slowly, twice.

"Right," he said. "Well. You'd know."

* * *

They were fifty minutes over. He stood when she stood.

"Jess," he said. "Sorry. Ms Heart. Thanks for coming out. I mean it. It's a long way for nothing much."

"It's not for nothing. I'll have the committal papers in a fortnight and I'll come back."

"You don't have to."

"I know."

He put his hand out, and then remembered, and took it back. He turned to the door and stood with his hands loose at his sides and waited to be taken.

* * *

She did it the other way round on the way out. The doors, the wait between them, the locker, the key.

In the car park the wind was coming up off the water and it was colder than town.

She wrote her note before she started the engine, because she had learned at twenty-four that you lose half of it on the drive. It took four minutes and it was a good note, and it recorded that the client presented as cooperative, understood the charges, and had been advised against raising the complainant's mental health.

Then she sat with the pen in her hand and looked at the fence going up over the hill.

He had been in that room for two hours and he had not once raised his voice. He had asked about Dean's kids. He had apologised for offering her a cup of tea.

And there were forty-one photographs in a sleeve in a cabinet in Macquarie Street, and she had read every page of the sixty-one, twice.

She could not get the two of them into the same man.

She started the engine.

Chapter 4

The Pipeline Track

Prue Dennison had been in Jess's life since they were nineteen, when they shared a house in Sandy Bay with a bath you had to light with a match.

They had a Saturday. It had survived two of Prue's pregnancies, one of Jess's bad years, a decade of night rosters and about four hundred Saturdays when one of them had cancelled. The rule was that cancelling did not count against you. The rule was also that you rang, and said why, and Jess had not rung.

"Four days," Prue said, in the car park at Fern Tree, pulling a beanie down over her ears. "Four days to return a call. I was going to send someone round."

"I was going to ring you tonight."

"You were going to ring me tonight," Prue said, "at which point I would have been asleep, because I have done three nights in a row and I am held together with Panadol and spite. Walk."

* * *

The track was flat and wet and went along the side of the mountain under the man ferns, with the old pipeline running beside it in and out of the bracken.

It had rained in the morning and everything was dripping without any rain falling. The light came down green.

"So the woman is forty-one," Prue said, "first baby, and she has a birth plan in a plastic folder. Laminated. And I say, lovely, we'll do our best, and she says, I've printed one for each of you."

"One each."

"One each. Four copies." Prue stepped over a root without looking at it. "And I want to be clear that I love her. I would die for that woman. But at ten past three in the morning, when it has all gone sideways and we're moving, her husband is standing in the corridor holding the folder up like it's a warrant."

"What happened?"

"Girl. Nine pound two. Everyone lived." She grinned. "And at about six she looks at me and says, I didn't do any of it the way it said. And I said, love, nobody ever does, that's not what the folder is for. The folder is so you've got something to hold in the car on the way in."

Jess laughed properly, which she had not done in about a fortnight.

They went another quarter of a mile. Her knees were complaining on the downhill bits in a way they had not at thirty, and she had worn the wrong bra for walking and had known it when she put it on.

"Your turn," Prue said.

"Nothing much."

"Jessica."

"I got a persistent. Legal aid. He's on remand." She watched where she was putting her feet. "It's a bad one."

"How bad?"

"Bad."

"Right." Prue walked for a while. "And you're the one standing up for him."

"Somebody has to. If nobody does it, then the only people who get a defence are the ones who can pay for one, and then you don't have courts, you've got a queue."

"I know. You've told me that since we were twenty-two and it's still true." A fantail came down and worked the air in front of them and went. "It wasn't what I asked, though."

"It was exactly what you asked."

"I asked how you are."

Jess did not say anything.

"Here's what I think," Prue said, "and then we'll have scones. I think you've never once in twenty years been able to leave a thing alone. Not the share house, not your dad, not the Corrigan business, not any of it. And I think you call it the job."

"It is the job."

"It isn't, though. The job is you turn up, you do the work properly, you go home at six and you have a life." Prue put her hands in her pockets. "That's what everyone else in that building does. You're the only one of them who still thinks the whole thing can be fixed."

"That's not what I think."

"All right."

"It isn't."

"All right," Prue said, and let it go, because she always let it go.

* * *

At the turnaround they sat on the wet rail and looked out over the trees to the water, a long way down and bright enough to squint at.

"Anyone on the scene?" Prue said.

"No."

"Since?"

"Marcus."

"Marcus was two years ago."

"Marcus was two years ago," Jess agreed.

"God." Prue considered this with her chin up. "I'm not going to say anything."

"You are, though."

"I'm going to say one thing and then I'm going to stop. You are thirty-six and you are very good company and there is nothing whatever the matter with you, and you have arranged your entire life so that nobody can get in the door."

"That's two things."

"It's one thing with a comma in it."

* * *

The house in South Hobart was warm and smelled of a roast and there was a bike in the hall that nobody had moved since Easter.

Gav had the football on with the sound off. Toby was twelve and wanted to show Jess a video of a man falling off a roof, and showed her four. Mia was fifteen and came out of her room for twenty minutes, which Prue said afterwards was a record for the year, and sat on the arm of Jess's chair and asked her — in a voice carefully got up to sound as though she did not care about the answer — whether you could do law if you were bad at maths.

"You can do law if you can read eleven thousand words and tell me what matters in them," Jess said.

"That's it?"

"That's most of it. The rest is being the one who actually read it."

Mia thought about that and went back to her room, and Prue caught Jess's eye across the kitchen and did not say anything at all, and poured her another glass.

They ate at four because of the night shift. Gav carved badly and apologised for it and nobody minded. Toby was sent to find the other serving spoon and came back with a wooden one and was sent again.

At the end Prue put a hand on the back of Jess's neck as she went past with the plates, briefly, the way she had been doing it since they were twenty.

* * *

It was dark by half past five.

Jess put her coat on in the hall and found Toby's bike with her shin, and said something, and from the kitchen Prue said that she was not to blame the bike.

"Next Saturday," Prue said at the door. "And if you don't ring me back I'm coming to the office. I'll sit in reception. I'll talk to people."

"You wouldn't."

"I would be delightful and you would never recover."

Jess got in the car.

Prue stood at the gate in her socks with her arms folded against the cold, and stayed there until the car turned the corner, the way she had done since they were nineteen.

Chapter 5

Complainant Disengaged

The committal papers came through on the eleventh, and with them the disclosure she had asked for and about a third of what she had actually wanted.

Hugh put his head round the door on the Tuesday.

"Curry. Where are we?"

"He hasn't instructed me on a plea."

"Get him to. It's a fixed fee and you've already done ninety hours on a grant that'll pay for thirty." He said it without malice, the way he said everything. "He's going down, Jess. The only live question is whether he gets any discount for it, and the discount shrinks every month you spend being thorough at him."

"I know what the discount does."

"I know you know." He tapped the doorframe twice and went.

He was right, and it was the correct advice, and it was what she would have told anybody else in the building to do.

* * *

She rang Kerry on the Wednesday, about character references.

It took four calls to find her. Not Kerry Curry any more; Kerry Bowden, at Sorell. The number came from a woman at the refrigeration business.

"Mrs Bowden, my name's Jessica Heart, I'm a solicitor in Hobart. I'm acting for Braden Curry and he's given me your name — he said you'd be happy to —"

The line went quiet.

Not a bad line. A person not saying anything.

"Mrs Bowden?"

"He said that."

"He said you were on good terms."

"Right," Kerry Bowden said.

And then she hung up — not angrily, not dramatically, just a woman putting a phone down on a kitchen bench somewhere and going back to whatever she had been doing.

Jess sat with the handset against her shoulder for a moment and then wrote the time of the call in the file, because you write down every call.

* * *

The 2011 application took her twenty minutes to find and about ninety seconds to read.

Interim family violence order, Magistrates Court at Hobart, on the application of Kerry Anne Curry. Granted on the fourth of May. Listed for hearing on the twenty-fifth. On the twenty-fifth there was a single line: application not pressed. Interim order lapsed.

Three pages. Nothing else on the file. No charge, no conviction, no mention of it anywhere in the antecedents that had been served on her, because there was nothing to mention. As far as the record was concerned, nothing had happened in 2011 to Braden Curry at all.

He had told her about Kerry himself. He had given her the name.

* * *

She went back into the brief that night with the office empty and the cleaners gone through.

At some point after eight she reached up under her blouse and unhooked her bra and pulled it out through the sleeve, because there was nobody left in the building and she was not going home yet.

It was at tab nine, in the police summary, in a paragraph she had read a fortnight ago and skimmed because it was background.

The complainant made a report at Bellerive on 9 March 2021 in relation to an assault alleged to have occurred on 6 March 2021. The complainant did not provide a formal statement. No further action was taken. The complainant disengaged.

Jess had read that phrase perhaps a thousand times in fourteen years. She had written it into submissions. She had stood up and said it out loud to magistrates, in exactly that tone, as a reason.

She found the entry in the running sheet behind it and put her finger on the dates.

Ninth of March, report taken at the front counter. Contact attempted, the eleventh. Contact attempted, the nineteenth. Complainant telephoned the station on the twenty-fourth of March, the second of April and the nineteenth of April. The file showed no returned call against any of the three. It said that she had not identified herself as a solicitor acting for the accused, then or at any time. It said that she had made no contemporaneous record of any of it. It said that she had signed a form at the Supreme Court of Tasmania on the ninth of June describing herself as a friend of the witness, and had sat with her for the whole of her evidence.

It asked for nothing. It did not say that she was no longer practising and it did not say that she was sorry, because neither of those was a fact that belonged in it.

At the bottom it said: I am available to answer any question the Board may have.

She read it on the Thursday and it was true and it was complete, and she sat back in the chair and felt something she had not felt since a corridor outside Court One in September.

* * *

She worked it out on the Thursday night, in bed, at about two.

She had not set out to work it out. She had been lying there going over a sentence, and the lawyer in her, which had not stopped once in five years, took the letter and ran it to the end.

The Board receives it. The Board must deal with it. There is an investigation and it is not secret.

And somewhere in that investigation somebody says out loud, in writing, that the solicitor on the record for Braden Curry was in a relationship with the complainant from before the committal, and sat beside her in the remote witness room while she gave her evidence.

That is a ground. That is a real ground. Jess lay in the dark and drafted the other side's submission in about ninety seconds, because she could have done it at twenty-six: the appellant's legal representatives were affected by an undisclosed conflict of such gravity that the trial miscarried.

Six years, non-parole three and a half, backdated to the fourteenth of June. He had been eligible since December.

And if it went anywhere at all — if a single judge thought there was something in it — then there would be a retrial, and there would be a witness list, and at the top of it there would be a name.

* * *

She did not delete the letter.

She got up at about three and sat in the kitchen in the dark and worked out, for the first time in five years, what she had actually done — which was not the café and not the car and not the form.

There was no honest act left that she could do on her own. Every one of them went through a witness room.

She could not fix it without spending Naomi to do it.

* * *

She took it to her on the Saturday morning.

That was the one decent thing in the whole chapter of her life and she would be able to hold onto it afterwards: she did not decide. She printed the letter and put it on the kitchen table and told her everything — the Board, the investigation, the appeal ground, the retrial, the witness list.

"Read it."

Naomi read it twice. It took her about four minutes.

"It's well written," she said.

"Naomi."

"No, I mean it. There's nothing in it feeling sorry for itself." She put it down and squared it with two fingers. "Is that what they'd do? An appeal?"

"They'd try. Any counsel worth paying would try."

"And would it work?"

"I don't know." She made herself say the rest of it. "It might. It's a good point. If I'm honest with you, it's the best point anybody's had in that case."

"And then I'd do it again."

"Yes."

"The room. The screen. All of it, with him sitting there." Naomi looked at the table for a while. "Who'd sit with me?"

* * *

Jess did not answer that, and Naomi did not make her.

She got up and filled the kettle and stood with her back to the room while it went, and Jess sat at the table with a page and three-quarters in front of her.

When she turned round her face had changed.

"Send it," she said.

"What?"

"Send it. If you need to send it, send it." She leaned back against the bench with her arms folded. "I mean that. I'll go back in the box. I've done it once and it nearly killed me and I'd do it again, and I'd be all right, because I'm not the woman who was in that flat with one chair any more."

"You don't —"

"Don't tell me what I don't understand." Steady. "I've sat in more courtrooms than you'd think. I know what it costs. And I'm telling you that if you have to do this to be able to live in your own head, then I will stand in front of that man one more time and I will not blame you for one day of it, not one, for the rest of my life."

The kettle came up behind her and clicked off.

"I'd rather have you," Naomi said, "than be comfortable."

* * *

Here is what Jess could have done on that Saturday.

She had thirty-four thousand dollars. She had a flat in her own name with eleven years on it, and the woman at the bench had no claim on it and would not have made one. She had a brain that two silks had called the best in the building. She was thirty-nine years old and in good health and the bus to Hobart went past the end of the street every twenty minutes.

And there was a woman in South Hobart who had said, two and a half years earlier, standing in socks at a gate: any day, any time, three in the morning, and that does not come off the table, ever.

She could have put four things in a bag on the Saturday afternoon and been drinking tea in Prue Dennison's kitchen by five o'clock.

She thought about all of it. She wants that on the record. She sat at that table and thought about every single piece of it.

* * *

What she did instead was stand up and go across the kitchen and put her arms round a woman who had just offered to be cross-examined for her.

"I'm not sending it," she said.

"Jess —"

"I'm not sending it. It's not a sacrifice and I'm not being noble." She had her face against the side of Naomi's head. "It would wreck one life and it wouldn't repair anything. It'd put you back in a room with him so that I could feel clean."

"You'd be able to sleep."

"I don't want to sleep that much."

And Naomi held on to her, very hard, for a long time, and said into her shoulder: "You are the best person I have ever met in my life."

* * *

She deleted the file that afternoon and emptied the bin and took the printed copy out to the kitchen bin under the sink and then took it out again and tore it into six pieces before she put it back.

Then she went and sat in the garden, and it was a good afternoon, and something had come off her.

She did not notice at the time that she had just done, to herself, in one morning, exactly what the hallway and the trackpad and the carpet at Taroona had all done, and that the difference was that this time nobody had asked.

* * *

On the Sunday she went to the drawer in the bedroom for her passport, because there was a renewal notice and because that is the sort of thing you do on a Sunday.

The passport was there. The spare keys were there.

The envelope flap was not.

She stood with the drawer open for a second and then went out to the lounge room, where Naomi was on the floor with the newspaper, and said, lightly, in the voice you use:

"Where'd that bit of envelope go?"

"I put it somewhere safe," Naomi said, without looking up.

And Jess stood in the doorway in her socks with her passport in her hand and opened her mouth to ask the next question.

She turned forty in the March and they had dinner at a place in Salamanca and it was a good night.

Then there was a winter, and then another spring, and the thing that is hardest to set down about that year is that most of it was pleasant. They walked. Naomi had four days at the council now and was good at it and was liked. There was a Wednesday class and a Thursday film and a woman who came to the door about a hedge.

Jess read. She read more in that year than she had in the eighteen before it. She would not have been able to tell you what about.

* * *

The flat came up in October.

Naomi had it worked out. Battery Point was forty-one years old and had no insulation and a bathroom from 1994 and the body corporate had put a special levy on in July. There were places at Lindisfarne and Howrah for two hundred and sixty thousand less with a yard, and she had been to see one.

"You'd be out from under it," she said. "No mortgage at all. Nothing owing on anything, ever again."

"And the rest of it goes where?"

"In the account. Where else would it go?"

Jess sat at the table and looked at her own hands.

"No," she said.

* * *

It was not like the other no.

The other no, in June of the fourth year, had been four seconds and flat and Naomi had been proud of her. This one came out of her like something tearing.

"That flat is mine," she said. "I bought it when I was twenty-seven on my own and it is the last thing in the world that is mine, and I'm not selling it, and I'm not putting the money in an account I can't get into."

The room went very quiet.

"You can't get into," Naomi said.

"You know what I mean."

"I'm asking what you mean."

"I don't know the password," Jess said. "I haven't known the password since April of the second year."

Naomi sat completely still for a moment. Then she got up and went and got the laptop and brought it back and put it on the table and turned it round and opened the banking page and typed, and the balance came up — twenty-nine thousand, four hundred and ten — and she pushed the laptop across.

"Change it."

"Naomi —"

"Change it now. Put whatever you like. Don't tell me what it is." Her chin had gone. "And then ring them in the morning and take my name off, I'll sign whatever they want, and I'll go back to doing my own and you can do yours, and —"

She stopped and put her hand over her mouth and stood up and went out of the room.

* * *

She was in the bathroom for twenty minutes and she came out with her face washed and she was perfectly all right, and she did not mention the account again, that night or ever.

And she did not change. She was not cold. She did not go away.

She grieved.

That was the word for it and Jess could not get round it. For about five weeks there was a woman in the flat who was quiet and kind and who flinched, very slightly, about once a day, when Jess came into a room. Who said of course to things. Who stopped putting her hand on Jess's back and then, when Jess said something about it, put it back on deliberately, which was worse than it being gone.

Jess apologised three times in five weeks for things she could not name, and on the third time Naomi cried and said, "Please stop saying sorry to me. You've done nothing. I'm the one who —" and did not finish it.

* * *

She went to the bank on the eleventh of November.

She did it quietly and she felt filthy doing it, and she queued behind a man with a cheque book and sat down with a young woman called Erin who had a lanyard and a screen.

It took eleven minutes.

The account was in both names and had been since April of the second year. Either party could operate it. There was twenty-nine thousand, four hundred and ten dollars in it, and Erin reset the online access there and then with Jess's licence and a code to her phone, and said that she could transfer the lot to a sole account that afternoon if she wanted and that it was entirely her decision.

There were no restrictions of any kind on that account. There never had been. Nothing had been hidden and nothing had been moved and every transaction for five years was there to be scrolled through, and Jess scrolled through nine months of it standing at a bench in a bank in Elizabeth Street and found a woman buying groceries and paying a mortgage and putting forty dollars a week in from a council wage.

"Was there anything else I can help with today?"

"No," Jess said. "Thank you. You've been very helpful."

* * *

She sat in the car in the car park at Centrepoint for a long time, which was where she had sat with her hair done five years before.

She had twenty-nine thousand dollars and access to all of it and a flat in her own name and a car with eleven months' rego on it.

And she sat there and could not think of one single place to drive.

* * *

She found the box on the twenty-eighth of November, and she was not looking for it.

The hot water service had gone and the plumber wanted the meter box key, and the meter box key was in the laundry cupboard on the top shelf with the paint tins and the bag of pegs and a plastic tub that had been there for years.

She got the tub down to reach past it and the lid came off.

* * *

It was not hidden. That was the first thing. It was a two-dollar plastic tub on a shelf in a laundry in a flat she owned.

Inside it, in order, there was this.

A spiral notebook with a blue cover, about half full, in Naomi's handwriting. The first page was dated the eighth of October of the first year and said: White Corolla bottom of Pindari St 0720–0755. Not a neighbour. Didn't get out. The second entry was the Wednesday with a registration number on it. The third was the Thursday.

The entries ran, roughly weekly, for five years. They were short. They were almost all ordinary — J home late, good mood, said the Lowther matter is settled — and perhaps one in fifteen was not ordinary, and those ones were dated and timed and had no adjectives in them at all.

23 July. Told J about 2017. J told me about Oct, all of it, incl the car. Produced envelope. 11.40pm.

Under the notebook there was a manila folder.

In it: a printout of a Supreme Court remote witness room support person form, which must have been photographed, because Jess had signed only one and had handed it to an officer. A printout of the Legal Profession Board's complaints page from a Thursday in September. A printout of the practice rule about communicating with a represented party. Two pages from a judgment with a paragraph marked in pencil.

And at the bottom, flat, in a small clear sleeve, a torn-off envelope flap with brown edges and an eight-year-old crease.

Naomi. Lindisfarne.

* * *

Jess sat down on the floor of the laundry with the tub in her lap.

Here is what she understood, in about ninety seconds, sitting on the lino with the plumber's van idling outside.

That this was the behaviour of a woman who had spent eleven years being told, pleasantly, each time, what she had actually meant — and who had come out the other side having learned exactly one thing, which was to write it down at the time.

That there was nothing in that tub that a reasonable person could call a weapon. That the first three entries were a frightened woman recording a car outside her house, and that she had been right to.

That the printouts were what anybody does at two in the morning.

That if this tub were put in front of a court, every single page of it would help Naomi and none of it would help Jess.

And that it was all true. That was the part. There was not one false line in the whole of it. A woman had written down, accurately, for five years, what the other woman in the flat had done.

* * *

She put the lid back on and put it on the shelf and gave the plumber the key and made him a cup of tea.

* * *

She left at twenty past eleven that night.

Naomi was asleep. Jess packed in the dark in the spare room with the door shut, and what she took was: three days of clothes, her passport, her birth certificate, the title documents from the bottom drawer, her toothbrush, the charger, a photograph of her father, and the grey bra from behind the thermals.

She stood in the bedroom doorway for a while and looked at a woman asleep on her side with one knee drawn up, in a bed in a flat at Battery Point, and did not wake her, and did not leave a note.

She drove to a motel on the Brooker and paid a hundred and twelve dollars for a room with a kettle and a plastic cup in a bag, and she lay on top of the covers in her coat with the light on and did not sleep at all.

Her phone did not go. That was the thing she kept checking for and it did not happen. At eight in the morning there was one message.

Your car's gone. I'm not going to ring you and I'm not going to come and find you. Whatever you need. x

* * *

She went to Bellerive.

She could not have told anybody why that one. There is a station in Hobart four minutes from the motel and she drove across the bridge and up and parked outside the station at Bellerive and sat there for twenty-five minutes.

Then she went in.

It is a small front counter with a glass partition and a rack of pamphlets and a bench along one wall, and there was a woman ahead of her about a dog, and then there was a constable of about twenty-six with a very clean uniform who said, "Morning. How can I help?"

And Jess Heart, who had stood up in that building's courtrooms for eighteen years, opened her mouth.

"I want to report —"

She stopped.

Because the next word had to be a noun, and there wasn't one.

* * *

She tried for about four minutes. She could hear herself doing it.

She said that she had been in a relationship for six years. She said that it had ended last night. She said that there was a — that there were photographs, that there were a lot of photographs, and the constable said, right, has she threatened to share those, and Jess said no.

She said that there had been a period where she had not had access to money, and the constable asked whether she had access now, and she said yes, and he said, right.

She said that she had given up her job, and he waited, and she said, no, nobody asked me to.

She said, "She knew who I was before she met me."

"Sorry — before she met you?"

"Before I met her. She knew." Jess heard it leave her mouth. "I was the solicitor acting for her ex-partner and she worked out who I was in the third week and she never said."

The constable looked at her.

"Okay," he said, carefully, and he was not unkind, and she watched him arrive at it. "And did she do anything to you? Physically?"

"No."

"Has she threatened you?"

"No."

"Has she said anything about the photos?"

"We used to joke about it," Jess said.

* * *

He took her name and date of birth and a contact number and he wrote about half a page.

Then he came round from behind the partition, which was decent of him, and gave her two pamphlets — one about family violence services and one with a number for a support line — and said that what she was describing sounded very difficult and that there were people a lot better at this than he was, and that somebody would be in touch.

"Can I ask what the follow-up is," Jess said.

"It'll go in as an occurrence and it'll get allocated." He was already half turned back to the computer. "Someone'll give you a ring."

She sat in the car outside the station at Bellerive with two pamphlets on the passenger seat.

Four hundred metres up the hill there was a café on a corner with nine tables and a window on two sides, where a woman in a navy coat used to sit in the chair that faced the door.

Nobody rang her that week.

Nobody rang her the week after.

She rang them in February and was told that the occurrence had been allocated, and in April that it had been reallocated, and in June a woman was very apologetic and took a message and read her number back to her twice.

In the last week of August she stopped ringing.

She had the folder done by then.

She had started it in the motel on the Brooker and finished it in a flat at Battery Point with no furniture in the lounge room, and it ran to sixty-four pages and it was the best piece of work she had done in five years.

Chronology. Tabs. A schedule of the money with the account numbers and the dates. The eleven drafts of the Board letter. A note of the tub in the laundry cupboard, its contents listed, undated, because she had no photograph of it and would not take one. Everything in order, counter-clockwise from her left hand, the way she had laid out every brief since she was twenty-three.

It was a good brief and she knew it was a good brief, and she also knew — sitting on the floor with it at one in the morning — that she had been reading briefs for nineteen years.

She made the appointment for the twenty-ninth.

The firm was a two-partner thing in a converted house at Lindisfarne that did family law and orders and conveyancing, and the woman she was given was about thirty and had been admitted four years and her name was Carly Deane.

It was three hundred and thirty dollars for the hour.

She asked to meet somewhere other than the office. She could not have explained why and Carly Deane did not ask her to, and said there was a café at Bellerive four minutes from them and would that do.

* * *

Jess got there twenty minutes early.

It had been painted since. There was a different machine and the counter had moved and the rack of newspapers had gone. There were still nine tables and a window on two sides, and the woman who had worked there eleven years was now a woman who had worked there seventeen, and she did not recognise her.

Jess bought a coffee and sat down and put the folder on the table.

* * *

She had thought about what to wear for two days.

The suits had gone to New Town three years ago. What she had was the navy coat and the grey coat and four good jumpers and the boots with the heel on them, and every single item of clothing she put on that morning had been chosen for her, in a shop, by the woman she had come to this café to make a complaint about.

She did her hair up with the pins.

She sat in the chair facing the door.

* * *

Carly Deane was small and quick and had a leather satchel and a laptop she did not open.

She was perfectly courteous and she was also, in a way Jess recognised at forty paces, slightly nervous — because she had looked the name up, obviously, the way anybody does, and three minutes of searching turns up eleven years of reported decisions with Ms J. Heart appeared for the accused in them.

"Thanks for meeting me out here. Shall I —"

"It's all in there," Jess said. "It's indexed. Tab one's the chronology."

"Right." Carly Deane put her hand flat on the cover and did not open it. "Can I do it the other way first? Just — tell me what's happened, in your own words, and then I'll read all of it properly, I promise. I'd rather hear it."

And that was the correct way to do it, and Jess had taught it to two juniors, and she sat there and could not get a sentence out for eleven seconds.

* * *

It took her fifty minutes.

She did it in order and she did it accurately and she did not editorialise, and at some point she noticed that she was giving evidence and could not stop.

Carly Deane wrote it down and asked eight questions.

*Had there been any physical violence at any time?* No.

*Any threat, express or implied, of physical violence?* No.

*Had the respondent ever prevented her from leaving a room, a house, a car?* No.

*The photographs and the recordings — had there been any threat to publish, share, or show them to anybody?* No. The opposite. She offered to destroy all of them, in front of me, and I said no.

*The money.* There were no restrictions. I could have walked into any branch at any time for five years. I didn't know the password because I never asked for it.

*Employment.* Nobody asked me to resign. She told me three times not to do it for her, once in terms, and I said at the time that I wasn't.

*The friends and the family.* I did that. Both of them. I made those phone calls myself and I was the one who said the unforgivable things.

*Taroona.* She asked me whether I wanted to stop, out loud, in front of two other people, and I said no.

* * *

Carly Deane stopped writing and sat back.

"Can I ask you a lawyer question or would you rather I didn't."

"Ask it."

"Why didn't you leave?"

"I had thirty-four thousand dollars, a flat in my own name, a car, and a friend in South Hobart who'd told me the door was open at three in the morning." Jess turned her cup round. "There isn't an answer. I've had nine months and there isn't one."

"That's not true, though, is it."

"Isn't it?"

"You've just given me the answer for fifty minutes," Carly Deane said. "You've just told me that she asked you every time, and that every time you said yes, and that each yes made the next one smaller. That's not nothing. That's a known thing. It's got a name."

Something went through Jess's chest.

"It has," she said.

"So I'm not going to sit here and tell you nothing happened to you."

* * *

And then she did the job properly.

She said that the definition was wide. That family violence in this state is not only assault — that economic abuse is in there, and emotional abuse and intimidation are in there, and that a course of conduct can found an order without anybody ever being hit. She said she had run those applications and won them.

She said: so the question is not whether the law has a word for it. The law has a word for it.

The question is what a magistrate would have in front of them on the day.

"Tell me what you think she'd file," Jess said.

"You tell me. You've read more of these than I have."

So Jess set it out, in a café at Bellerive, acting for the other side one last time.

A response denying nothing of consequence, because nothing of consequence needed denying. Bank statements for five years showing a joint account with no restrictions, a woman putting forty dollars a week in from a council wage, and nine thousand dollars saved in the first four months. A resignation letter in Jess's own words. Two witnesses from Taroona who would say, truthfully, that the applicant was asked whether she wanted to stop and said no. A support person form signed Friend in the applicant's hand.

And a spiral notebook with a blue cover, begun on the eighth of October eight years ago, with White Corolla bottom of Pindari St 0720–0755 on the first page.

"Yes," Carly Deane said quietly. "That's what I'd have filed."

* * *

"And then there's the other thing," she said, "and I have to put it to you, because if I don't, somebody will."

"Go on."

"If she applied against you. What would she have?"

Jess sat with her hands round a cold cup.

"She'd have a solicitor who obtained her address from the brief of a man charged with persistently abusing her," she said. "Who attended her street on three mornings and recorded none of it. Who engineered a meeting and did not identify herself. Who formed a sexual relationship with her before the trial and concealed it from the court, her principal, counsel and the Director. Who sat beside her through her evidence on a form describing herself as a friend."

"Yes."

"She'd have all of that from my own mouth, in writing, eleven times over, in tab seven."

"Yes."

"And a magistrate would hear it in open court," Jess said, "and would be right to."

* * *

The machine went behind the counter and somebody laughed at the far end of the room.

"I can file it," Carly Deane said. "It's your decision, not mine. If you tell me to file, I'll file it, and I'll run it properly, and I won't pull a punch."

"But."

"But I'd be putting you in a witness box for two days to be cross-examined about every one of those answers you gave me, by somebody good, and at the end of it my honest view is that you would not get your order." She said it without dropping her eyes. "And it would be the only time any of this is ever said out loud in a public courtroom, and it would be said about you."

"I know."

"I'm sorry. I'd rather tell you that now than in nine months."

Nine months, Jess thought.

* * *

Here is what she had said, standing up, in Court 3 and Court 1 and a dozen others, for eighteen years, in exactly that tone, as a reason.

The complainant disengaged.

* * *

Carly Deane put the folder back across the table, squared it with two fingers, and did not let go of it straight away.

"Can I say one more thing, and it's not legal advice."

"Yes."

"The best brief I've ever seen is the one you've just handed me about yourself." She let go of it. "That's not a kind thing to say. I think it's a terrible thing, actually. But somebody should tell you that you're still very good at this."

* * *

She paid the three hundred and thirty dollars by transfer at the table, which took about forty seconds, and Carly Deane packed the satchel and stood up and then stopped.

"Is there someone you can ring?"

And Jess looked past her, at the door.

She had been sitting in that chair for an hour and a half and had not once thought about why she had chosen it. It faces the door. You can see everybody who comes in and everybody who goes out and nobody can come up behind you, and a woman had sat in it eight years ago with a book she was not reading and a bag on the seat opposite, and Jess had put that down to being frightened, and had been right.

"Yes," she said. "There is."

* * *

She sat on in the café for about twenty minutes after Carly Deane had gone.

Then she took her phone out and found the number. Underneath it the app said that the last call between them was five years and ten months ago.

It rang four times.

"Jess?"

She opened her mouth and nothing came out of it at all, not one word, and she sat in a chair facing a door with her hand over her eyes while a woman in a kitchen in South Hobart waited.

"Right," Prue said. "Where are you."
