Chapter 1

The Transit

The cabin was sunk in the blue half-light of the descent into Milan.

From her aisle seat in row forty-two, Sharon Taylor watched the back of the man three rows ahead. She had been watching him since Dubai. He was large, fifty-something, silver-grey hair barbered neat at the collar, in a charcoal jacket that had cost money and gave him no comfort. Even asleep, his shoulders were hunched forward.

Sharon took a sip of her mineral water. For thirty years, in a cold house in Glenorchy, she had lived with Trevor, who counted the change in her purse on Fridays to see whether she had bought herself a decent cut of meat. Trevor was dead. The insurance money sat in her account, and she was fifty-five, and she was wearing a navy poly-cotton blouse and elastic-waisted slacks from a Hobart department store, and she was going to Italy.

She knew men. She knew how the pride worked, and the anger, and how the tiredness got into the bones and stayed there.

The man three rows ahead was not a Trevor. He was a beast of burden. She watched him rub his face with both hands as the lights came up amber.

Three rows ahead, Warren stared at the flight map on the screen in front of him. Fifty-seven. Thirty years of coastal land, and then more money after Elizabeth's illness went on and on and finally stopped. Elizabeth had been a good woman. She had turned the marriage into a fortress of beige routine, and he had been the provider, the rock, the one who decided.

He didn't want to decide anything else. He wanted to lay the steering wheel down.

The aircraft banked. The gear came down with a shudder that went through the floor.

They did not speak in the cabin. They did not speak in the corridors at Malpensa, or in the immigration queue.

At Carousel 8 he wrestled a canvas suitcase off the belt. His fingers slipped on the handle and the bag hit the floor three inches from her shoes.

Sharon's hand was at her collarbone before she knew it had moved. She put it down again.

"Apologies." His voice was gravel from the dry air. He straightened up. "Clumsy of me. The joints stiffen after twenty hours, and I've the drive up to the lakes yet."

Sharon didn't step back. "Lake Como?"

"Bellagio." He rubbed the back of his neck. "Somewhere quiet for a few days."

She took in the watch, the lines at his eyes, the slump.

"No harm done," she said. "But you look like a man who has been carrying a heavy load for a very long time."

Warren froze.

"I suppose I have." It was out before he could stop it. "Warren. Warren Thornhill. Byron Bay."

"Sharon. Hobart." She did not offer her hand. She took a notepad and a pen from her handbag, wrote a sequence of numbers, tore the page free and held it out.

"I'm going to Bellagio myself. If you find you want to put that steering wheel down for an afternoon, send me a message. We'll have coffee."

Chapter 2

The Bellagio Encounter

For two days Warren walked the cobbled salitas of Bellagio like a ghost haunting a postcard.

The air smelled of pine and espresso. The lake glittered under the late August sun. It was the trip Elizabeth had planned and saved for over a decade, and she had died before they could board the flight. Now he was here, executing the itinerary of a dead woman from a polished suite at the Grand Hotel Villa Serbelloni.

On the afternoon of his third day he sat at a marble-topped table at a lakeside cafe with a Campari and soda he did not want.

Beside the glass lay the torn slip of paper from Carousel 8.

He was a sensible man. He did not chase women, and he did not invite strange Australian widows he had met in baggage halls to coffee. He looked at the numbers for a long time.

His fingers tapped the screen.

Sharon. It's Warren from the airport. I am at Cafe Sanremo by the ferry terminal. The steering wheel is getting heavy.

He pressed send and expected to wait an hour. Perhaps a day.

The reply came in ninety seconds.

Order me a pot of Earl Grey. I am walking down from the basilica now.

When she appeared on the promenade ten minutes later he stood up so quickly his knee knocked the iron table leg. She was not dressed for the Riviera. She wore the same navy slacks and a dusty-rose blouse that had come off a rack in Hobart five years ago, and she came through the crowded terrace at a pace that made the linen and the sunglasses at the other tables look like a great deal of effort.

"Warren," she said as he pulled out a chair. "You found a good table. Out of the wind."

"It's good to see you." He lowered himself back down. He felt large, and clumsy, and very close to the table.

She folded her hands on the marble. She did not look at the lake.

"You haven't slept properly since Milan," she said. "In fact, looking at your jaw, I'd say you haven't slept properly in about ten years. Are you still trying to manage everything? Even on a holiday?"

Warren opened his mouth to laugh it off and the sound died in his throat.

"My wife passed away fourteen months ago," he said. "She was ill a long time. I handled the business, the nurses, the estate, the garden. I handled all of it." He turned his glass by the rim. "I came here because it was her dream trip. But I'm so bloody tired, Sharon. I wake up and the thought of deciding what to do next feels like carrying lead."

The waiter came with a silver teapot and a cup. She said nothing until he had gone.

She poured.

"Elizabeth, I presume."

Warren blinked. "Yes. How did you—"

"Men who look like you usually marry Elizabeths. Or Margarets, or Susans." She took a slow sip. "Good women. Sensible women. Women who make austerity a virtue and run the house on a timetable. I imagine she picked out that charcoal jacket because it wouldn't show the dirt."

His face went red. "She was very practical."

"I know she was." No judgment in it at all. "Just as I know my Trevor was a bully who counted the pennies in my purse every Friday. We both played our parts. You played the stoic provider. I played the obedient mouse."

She set the cup down. The china clicked against the saucer and it was the loudest thing on the terrace.

"But Trevor is dead," she said. "And Elizabeth is dead. The play is over. The question is, Warren, why are you still wearing the costume?"

The breeze off the lake moved the collar of his shirt.

"The costume," he said. "You think I'm still performing."

"I think you don't know how to stop. You've spent thirty years being the rock. You made the money, you maintained the property, you managed the illness. And now you're sitting on an Italian lake, a wealthy free man, and you look like you're waiting for someone to hand you a shovel."

She leaned forward, forearms on the table. The faded rose cotton pulled across her shoulders.

"You said the steering wheel is getting heavy. But you don't want to pull over for a rest, do you. You want someone else to drive."

Warren looked out at the ferry churning the water white. The urge to deny it came up and went down again.

"I don't want to decide anything anymore," he said. "I don't want to decide where to eat, or what to wear, or where to go. I want to be told. I want to put it down, Sharon. All of it."

He braced for pity, or disgust.

When he looked back she was smiling. It was a small smile, the kind a mother wears when a fever breaks.

"I know, dear," she said. "And it is perfectly alright to want that."

She put her hand over his. Her palm was cool and her grip was firm.

"But surrender isn't something you can wish into existence over a cup of tea. It has to be practised. It has to be structured. You need a framework to take the weight off those shoulders."

"How?"

"By starting small." She withdrew her hand and sat back, and gestured down at her own blouse. "We are both wearing the armour of our past lives. Trevor made sure I never spent more than twenty dollars on a garment. I promised myself on the flight over that I would leave this Tasmanian ghost behind and build a new wardrobe from the skin out."

She looked out at the water.

"Though given Trevor's refusal to invest in anything but cheap beer and bad cars, what he left was painfully modest. My reinvention will have to be a slow, budgeted affair. One good piece at a time."

Warren looked at the faded rose cotton. Then he thought of his own accounts, and the seven figures sitting untouched and gathering interest, and of Elizabeth agonising for a week over a mid-range hotel room.

This was something he understood. He could provide. It was a familiar role, and it asked nothing of him but money.

"Sharon," he said. "Let me."

"Let you what, Warren?"

"Let me fund it. All of it. Today."

"Warren, don't be ridiculous. We only just met at—"

"I am a very wealthy man." The words came out in a rush. "Between the land on the coast and Elizabeth's life insurance, I have more money than I could spend in two lifetimes. I never have to work again. But that money feels like lead. It's a monument to thirty years of saving."

He took the black titanium card out of his breast pocket and laid it flat on the marble between them.

"You want to step out of that old life. And I want to stop carrying the weight of mine. Let me carry the bags. Let me pay for the silk. Give me the privilege of being useful to you, without having to make a single decision."

Sharon looked at the card on the marble. She did not reach for it. She studied it for a long moment, and then she looked up at his face, and what had been warm in her eyes went somewhere else.

"That is a very significant surrender," she said. "Are you certain?"

"I've never been more certain of anything."

She nodded slowly. She still did not reach for it.

"Put that back in your pocket for now, dear." She stood and smoothed the creases from her slacks. "You can handle the transactions at the counter. Let's start with the foundations. I saw a lingerie boutique on the salita just up from the hotel."

Warren stood. He slipped the card back into his pocket and followed her out through the tables.

Chapter 3

The Boutiques

The salita rose sharply from the lakefront, a narrow avenue of old cobbles between high stone walls and the glass fronts of the boutiques.

For the first time in fourteen months Warren was not carrying the weight of the day. He followed half a step behind her with his shoulders loose and his mind quiet. No map to check. No itinerary. No reservations to confirm. He followed the line of her navy slacks through the crowd.

The first stop was a lingerie boutique in amber light that smelled of bergamot.

Sharon did not browse the way a woman browses when she is checking the tags. She went to the counter and spoke to the saleswoman in clipped English, and inside ten minutes she had chosen four sets of silk and French lace in blush, ivory and emerald, sheer hosiery, and a floor-length silk robe.

When the tissue paper was folded, Warren stepped forward and tapped his card against the terminal. The sum was more than Elizabeth had spent on clothes in five years. The terminal beeped.

He gathered the bags by their corded handles. "Next?"

"Leather goods," Sharon said. "And then tailoring."

For three hours they replaced Sharon Taylor of Glenorchy piece by piece. In a shop near the basilica she was fitted for cream wool slacks, an ivory silk blouse and a tailored navy blazer. In a leatherworker's she chose calfskin loafers and a structured Italian handbag.

Warren paid at every counter. He produced the card, tapped it, and gathered the bags into his hands. He was the packhorse. It was the most useful he had felt in a year.

Walking back down, he had half a dozen bags and the handles were biting into his fingers, and he was sweating under the charcoal jacket.

Halfway down the cobbles, Sharon stopped.

She stood at the window of a jeweller, in front of the gold and the pearl and the cut stones on black velvet, looking at a thin gold chain. Her reflection lay over the top of it.

Warren shifted the bags on his wrists. "Let's go inside."

She stepped back from the glass. "Don't be ridiculous. We've spent enough of your money today. Besides, I haven't had a new piece of jewellery since I married Trevor, and that was a discount ring he complained about for a decade. I wouldn't know what to do with any of it."

Warren set his jaw.

"Well, that settles it. I insist. You go in there and pick out two or three pieces. More, if you like. I'm not having you wear that silk without something proper around your neck."

Sharon turned and looked at him — the flushed earnest face, the half-dozen paper bags hanging off his thick hands.

"You are a very stubborn man," she said.

She unclasped her new handbag and held it open between them.

"If you're going to insist, then put the card in here. Now. You look like a packhorse, and I will not have you fumbling with all those handles and dropping my new silk over a jeweller's cabinet when it comes time to pay."

It was a perfectly sensible domestic instruction.

Warren shifted two bags to his left wrist, reached into his breast pocket with his right hand, and dropped the card into the open compartment.

The brass clasp snapped shut.

"Thank you, dear. Wait out here in the fresh air. I won't be long."

Warren stood on the cobbles with her bags and watched her walk into the velvet-lined room. He watched her point into the case. The jeweller brought out a tray, and within five minutes she had a gold chain and a pair of pearl earrings.

He watched her take his card out of her handbag and hand it across the counter. The jeweller put it into the terminal, pressed something, and then looked up and gestured politely at the keypad.

Sharon came back to the door and opened it just enough to let the cool air spill onto the cobbles.

"Warren. The limit wants a PIN for an amount this size. You'll need to come in and enter it." She glanced at the bags hanging from his wrists. "Try not to knock over a cabinet on your way through the door."

Warren looked at the narrow entrance, and the glass, and the bags biting into his fingers. Then he looked at her face, holding the door.

He didn't want to squeeze through the door. He didn't want to see the total on the screen. He didn't want to authorise it.

"No," he said. He stood where he was. "I trust you. You do it."

Sharon's hand stayed on the brass handle. "You are giving me the PIN to your primary account. Are you quite sure?"

"It's 1969," he said, without a second's hesitation. "Enter it yourself. Get whatever you want."

She looked at him for a long moment. The chatter and the coffee cups on the salita seemed to go a long way off.

"Nineteen sixty-nine," she said. "Stay right there, dear. I won't be another minute."

The glass door swung shut.

Warren stood alone on the cobbles. Through the window he watched her walk back to the counter and punch four digits into the terminal. The machine beeped. The receipt printed. She lifted her hair and clasped the gold chain around her neck in front of the mirror, and turned her chin to see it.

His wallet was still in his pocket.

Chapter 4

The Celebration

They did not go down to dinner. They carried the bags up to Sharon's suite.

The room was full of late gold light. Warren sat in the armchair by the window with a glass of mineral water and watched, for the next hour, as she came out of the bathroom in one thing after another.

She was discreet. She did not model the emerald robe or the lace, though she let the tissue-lined boxes sit open on the bed. "The foundations fit perfectly," she said, smoothing the front of the ivory blouse. "You have a generous eye."

She modelled the navy blazer, the cream slacks, the calfskin loafers. With the gold chain against her collarbones there was nothing left of the woman from the baggage hall.

"I need to use the facilities," Warren said, standing up. "Just a moment."

He went into the en suite and pulled the door shut.

The moment the latch clicked, Sharon moved.

She opened the bottom drawer of the dresser, where she had unpacked her old life, pulled out a heavy paper shopping bag, and began filling it. In went the frayed ribbed singlets. In went the beige 38D bras. In went the elastic-worn cotton briefs. Then she reached up under the new cream slacks, slid off the plain white knickers she had walked the cobbles in all day, and dropped them on top of the pile.

She folded the bag shut as the plumbing rushed behind the door.

When Warren came out she was by the door with her handbag over one shoulder and the rolled paper bag in her hand.

"A change of scenery, I think," she said, adjusting the lapels of the blazer. "Let's go over to your room. We can drop this rubbish in your bin on the way."

They walked the carpeted corridor to Room 24. Warren unlocked the door and held it for her. His canvas bags sat where he had left them by the luggage rack.

Sharon walked to the centre of the room and turned, her fingers at the gold links.

"This needs to be celebrated," she said. "I haven't felt this alive in thirty years. Go down to the bar and buy us a bottle of proper champagne. The best they have on ice."

She opened her handbag, took out his card and held it out to him.

Warren took it. This was his domain — the provider, fetching the wine. "I won't be ten minutes."

The door clicked shut behind him.

She went to the armoire and tipped the paper bag upside down onto the bed.

Then she turned to the cedar shelves. On the centre shelf sat Warren's folded underwear. Ribbed cotton boxer shorts. Thick white undershirts.

With two sweeps of her arm she cleared the shelf into the empty bag.

She gathered her own discarded life off the bed and laid it out on the bare cedar. She stacked the singlets. Beside them she laid the beige bras, the wide straps tucked neatly under. Then the washed-out briefs, and on the very top of the pile, squarely, the worn pair she had taken off an hour ago.

She took a sheet of hotel stationery from the desk and wrote three lines.

She propped the note against the stack and pulled the cedar doors shut.

She was in the armchair by the window with her legs crossed and her hands in her lap, the rolled paper bag by her feet, when Warren came back.

He had a green bottle of Bollinger and two crystal flutes. He was flushed and happy. He set the glasses down, poured, handed her one, and drank.

He reached into his pocket and held the card out to her.

She took it from his fingers and dropped it into her handbag.

"To new beginnings, Warren."

"To new beginnings."

They finished the champagne as the sun went down behind the mountains.

When the clock in the hall struck the quarter-hour she set her flute on the desk and stood, and picked up her handbag, and then the paper bag from the floor.

"I'm going to have the concierge book us a table by the water for eight," she said. "Have a shower and get changed for dinner."

"I'll see you downstairs."

Sharon stepped into the corridor and pulled the door shut until the latch caught. She walked down the wide stairs to the lobby. Beside the velvet curtains stood a tall brass bin. Without breaking stride she dropped the paper bag into it and went on to the concierge desk.

Upstairs, Warren finished the last of the Bollinger. He stripped off the charcoal jacket, unbuttoned his shirt, stepped out of his trousers and boxers and left them in a heap, and stood a long time under the hot water.

With a towel around his waist he came back into the bedroom and opened the double doors of the armoire to get dressed.

The shelf was bare, except for a stack of faded women's underwear, and a sheet of hotel stationery propped against it.

The handwriting was unhurried.

A new beginning requires new foundations. Wear these tonight. — S.

Warren dropped the paper and tore open his canvas bag by the door. Empty. He checked the drawers. Empty.

He had no underwear. And she had his card.

He went back to the armoire. On top of the pile was the elastic-worn pair she had been wearing all day. Beside it a white ribbed singlet, and tucked under that, the beige 38D bra.

He was fifty-seven years old. He was wealthy. He was respected.

He picked up the worn cotton knickers and stepped into them and pulled them up his thighs. The elastic bit at his waist. He pulled the singlet over his head and the jersey clung to his chest.

He looked at the bra on the cedar.

No. Not to dinner. I can't wear a bra under a dinner shirt. I draw the line.

He left it on the shelf. He pulled on a fresh dress shirt and his charcoal trousers and buttoned them fast, and every time he shifted his weight the elastic moved against his hips.

Chapter 5

The First Layer

The restaurant was candlelit and smelled of roasted garlic and lake water.

Sharon was already seated. The gold chain caught the candlelight against the ivory silk. Warren sat down opposite her, stiff, careful, certain that the trim of the singlet was showing at his collar.

She ordered the wine and the antipasti without asking him. She handed the menu back, folded her hands, and looked at him. The flat drape of his shirt told her he had left the bra on the shelf, and she said nothing about it. That was a battle for another day.

"You look uncomfortable," she said. "Is the chair too hard?"

Under the heavy fabric of his trousers the elastic was digging in.

"The chair is fine." He looked at the tablecloth. "Sharon. The wardrobe. My things."

"Your things are gone." She might have been confirming the weather. "You told me this afternoon you wanted to stop carrying the weight of your old life. You gave me your card to prove it. I am taking you at your word, dear."

She leaned forward. The candle caught the edge of something in her face.

"Tell me the truth. We aren't play-acting. How does it actually feel?"

Warren closed his eyes.

"It feels shameful," he said. "I'm sitting in a good restaurant and underneath my clothes I'm wearing a woman's cast-offs. I feel ridiculous. I feel like you've taken every piece of dignity I had left."

"And yet?"

He opened his eyes.

"And yet I don't want to go upstairs and take them off." His voice was thick. "I feel trapped. But it's the quietest my mind has been in thirty years. I feel like I'm finally allowed to be small."

Sharon smiled.

"That is the paradox of surrender," she said, reaching across to pat his hand. "The ego fights it. The soul craves it. You are wearing my past so that I can wear your future. It's a fair trade."

She picked up her wine.

"Now. Eat your dinner. Tomorrow we take the ferry to Menaggio, and we have a great deal of packing to do before then."

Chapter 6

The Ferry

Warren stood on the open deck as the ferry worked its way south towards Como.

He wore his charcoal trousers and his jacket and looked like a respectable Australian widower. Every time the boat rolled, his thighs shifted and the worn elastic bit at his hips. The singlet clung under his shirt.

He found he didn't mind the chafing. The pinch of the elastic held him in place. He watched the villas go by and wondered, without much urgency, what she was going to do with him next. He had no money. He had no cards. He was a passenger.

He went back inside to their table.

Sharon was looking out at the shoreline in her blazer and her cream slacks. Beside her untouched espresso lay a book.

She stood and smoothed her slacks. "I'm going out to the bow for some air before we dock. Watch my things."

She left the book on the table.

Warren looked at it. It was a paperback, and the cover was plain: The Architecture of Surrender. He opened it where she had folded down a corner.

A single line was underlined in black ink, twice.

the male ego does not shatter — it sighs in relief

He shut the book. Then he opened it again and read the line a second time, and put it back exactly where it had been, square to the edge of the table.

When Sharon came back she said nothing about it. She slid the book into her handbag, next to his card, and gestured for him to pick up the luggage.

An hour later they arrived at a small hotel on a cobbled square in Como.

Sharon handled the check-in while Warren stood behind her holding the canvas bags. The concierge gave her two brass keys and they were shown up to a suite on the top floor.

The layout was lopsided. To the left, double doors opened onto a master bedroom with a king bed and the lake. To the right, down a short hallway, was an annex with a narrow iron bed and a wooden dresser.

"Put my bags in the master," Sharon said, walking in and opening the balcony doors. "You can put yours in the small room at the back."

Warren carried the bags. He set his own on the narrow bed and came back.

She had already opened hers. The Bellagio wardrobe was laid out across the master bed — the emerald robe, the lace, the tailored blouses. Beside the pile sat the rolled paper bag of her old Tasmanian clothes.

Sharon stood at the foot of the bed holding up the lilac poly-cotton blouse and the navy elastic-waisted slacks. She ran her thumb over the pilled fabric.

"I have to thank you again for the silk," she said. "It feels magnificent against the skin. I don't think I could wear this Tasmanian rubbish again. But it seems a shame to throw them out. They're perfectly serviceable garments for someone doing the heavy lifting. Don't you think?"

Warren's throat closed. The underlined line came back to him, whole.

"Yes," he said. "I suppose they are."

Sharon smiled. "Good." She dropped the lilac blouse on the mattress. "Take off your jacket, Warren."

He stood with his hands hovering at his sides. "Now? Sharon, it's two in the afternoon."

"I am aware of the time. Take off the jacket. Unbutton the shirt. Put on the bra."

There was no night to prepare in. The afternoon sun came through the balcony doors and lay across the bed.

Warren slipped out of the jacket. He unbuttoned the shirt, let it fall, and stood in his trousers and the white singlet.

"The bra."

He picked up the beige 38D. He put his arms through the straps and had to strain, his back flexing, to get the hooks together over the singlet. The underwire bit in beneath his chest.

"Now the socks." She pointed at his bag in the annex.

Warren walked stiffly through, got two pairs of thick athletic socks, and came back. He pushed the rolled socks into the empty cups. He looked down. Two mounds, high on his chest.

"The slacks. And the blouse."

He stepped out of his trousers. He was standing in her knickers. He pulled the navy slacks up his legs; the crotch was shallow and the waistband cinched high and round over his hips.

Then the lilac blouse. His fingers were clumsy on the small mother-of-pearl buttons. As he fastened them, the stuffed cups pushed the thin cotton out in front of him.

"Leave your suit on the floor," Sharon said, picking up her handbag. "We're going down to the plaza for coffee."

Warren slid his feet into his heavy brown leather travel shoes and followed her out.

Chapter 7

The Plaza

The square was busy with tourists and the late sun was laying long shadows over the outdoor tables.

Warren walked half a step behind her with his shoulders hunched, trying to make less of the front of the lilac blouse. Every step felt like it belonged to somebody else.

Sharon chose a table at the edge of the plaza and ordered an espresso for herself and a mineral water for him. She sat straight in the navy blazer. Warren sat opposite with his knees pressed together, afraid the hem of the slacks would ride up.

The waiter set the drinks down and went away without looking at him twice.

Sharon took a sip of her espresso. She looked at his flushed face, and then her eyes went down, under the table.

Below the hem of the navy slacks, Warren's feet were in the scuffed brown leather travel shoes. They were enormous. They sat on the cobbles like two bricks.

Sharon set her cup down. The ceramic clicked against the saucer.

"Those shoes," she said, "are ridiculous."

Warren pulled his feet back under the chair. "They're my walking shoes. They're the only ones I have."

"Not for much longer. They look absurd under those slacks. You look like a man playing dress-up, and I will not tolerate half-measures. A servant does not wear brogues."

She finished her espresso and snapped the handbag shut.

"Drink your water. When we're done here we'll find a discount shoe shop, and we will buy you a pair of sensible wide-fit flats, and you will leave those boots in the shop's bin."

Warren looked down at the brown leather. They had carried him across Australia, through thirty years of marriage, and onto the flight to Milan.

He looked back up at her.

"Yes, Sharon," he said, and picked up his glass.

They walked two streets back from the water, away from the boutiques, into a shaded alley where the locals shopped.

The shop was cramped and brightly lit and smelled of rubber and floor wax. The walls were stacked with boxes. Warren came through the door and felt enormous in the narrow aisles, and kept his arms pinned to his sides.

A teenage assistant looked up from the counter, took in his shape, and looked away again.

Sharon did not browse. She went to a rack of discounted comfort shoes at the back and ran her eye along it until she found the largest sizes.

"Sit down, Warren." She pointed at a low plastic stool.

He lowered himself onto it. The shallow crotch of the slacks bit into him.

She pulled a box from the bottom shelf and opened it. Inside was a pair of wide-fit slip-on flats in dull black synthetic, with a rounded toe and a thin rubber sole. They were the shoes of tired waitresses and of women doing the Tuesday shopping.

"Take off the boots."

Warren reached down. His fingers fumbled at the waxed laces. He pulled the boots off and sat in his socks.

"Now try these."

He slid his right foot in. It was tight across the bridge, but the synthetic gave. He put the left one on.

He looked down. The brown leather was gone and there were two neat rounded black shoes on his feet, and a strip of pale ankle above each one.

"Stand up."

Warren stood. Without the heel and the thick sole he felt suddenly unmoored. He could feel the linoleum.

Sharon went to the counter, took his card out of her handbag, tapped it against the terminal, and came back. She pointed at a small metal bin beside the fitting stools.

"Drop the leather shoes in the bin. We're leaving."

Warren looked at the boots. He bent down, picked them up, and dropped them in. They hit the bottom with a hollow thud.

He followed her out onto the street.

The Instruction

They put her in the master suite. Not the room off the kitchen.

Chapter 32

The Statue

The pool lay on the lower terrace, behind a hedge of clipped box, with the olive groves falling away below it and no house in sight for three kilometres.

At the far end, on a plinth the property managers had never bothered to explain, stood a marble woman.

"Stand beside her," Sharon said.

"No."

"Neither does anybody else. She is a copy of a copy. She came a few steps closer. "Men made every one of them. She stopped in front of her.

"Thirty years I was in a house with a man who called me a cow when I dropped a fork," she said. "And the same species that made him carved her."

"That is what you are now," Sharon said. "You were a man who was frightened of deciding what to have for dinner. Now you are the thing they build on plinths. Not useful. Not sensible. Not a rock or a provider or a shovel. Something to be worshipped."

The water moved in the pool and put light up onto the underside of the olive leaves.

Chapter 33

The Fork

They stayed.

There was talk, in the second year, of Rome, and once of going back to look at the lake. Neither happened. The villa took them the way old houses take people — a room at a time, until the idea of being anywhere else required more organising than either of them was willing to do.

They went out. Not often, and never to prove anything. Twice a year or so they drove into Florence and ate somewhere with a view of the river, and Rowena wore the emerald silk, and men looked at her and she let them, and it stopped being an event somewhere around the fourth time. There was one evening in a bar near the Arno when a younger man was persistent enough that Rowena leaned across the table and kissed Sharon in front of him to be rid of him, and they laughed about it in the car. She had been pleased with herself for a week. She was less pleased, later, when she worked out that she had looked at Sharon's face before she did it, to check.

She looked at Sharon's face before most things.

She could not read Italian but she could conduct an entire transaction about tomatoes with the woman who came up from the village. Her hair went a paler blonde in summer. "There's another in the drawer," she said, and went back to the stove.

"Rowena."

"Yes."

"The drawer."
